Significance

While the benefits of childbearing typically accrue to both parents, the costs of childbearing come at the expense of women’s careers. We analyze roughly 100 times more sample than previous longitudinal survey datasets for the United States and document startling findings regarding the magnitude and pervasiveness of the motherhood penalty. The motherhood penalty persists and sometimes increases in contexts where a priori one might think it would fall, including firms with female leaders or heterosexual couples where the woman outearns the man. The persistent US penalty we document is surprising but consistent with previous research that argued that the motherhood penalty is driven by both cultural and economic factors.

Keywords: motherhood penalty, gender gaps, labor market inequality, LEHD

Abstract

Whereas previous research has described motherhood penalties in US survey data, we leverage administrative data on 811,000 quarterly earnings histories from the US Unemployment Insurance program. We analyze contexts where smaller motherhood penalties might be expected: couples where the woman outearns her male partner prior to childbearing, at firms that are headed by women, and at firms that are predominantly women. Our startling result is that none of these propitious contexts appear to diminish the motherhood penalty, and indeed, the gap often increases in magnitude over time following childbearing. We estimate one of the largest motherhood penalties in “female-breadwinner” families, where higher-earning women experience a 60% drop from their prechildbirth earnings relative to their male partners. Turning to proximate mechanisms, women are less likely to switch to a higher-paying firm postchildbearing than men and are substantially more likely to quit the labor force. On the whole, our findings are discouraging relative even to existing research on motherhood penalties.

While the benefits of childbearing accrue to both parents, the burden of childbearing comes at the expense of women’s labor market outcomes. The large and persistent gender inequality in the labor market more broadly increasingly results from the differential impacts of parenthood by gender (1–3).

For mothers, employment and earnings conditional on being employed fall sharply around the time of birth for women, and, more ominously, may remain permanently lower well after childbirth (1–8). For fathers, it is challenging to detect any labor market impact of childbearing.

Here, we leverage administrative data to focus on specific US contexts where the motherhood penalty should be attenuated. We can isolate these “propitious” contexts, thanks to the universal data on earnings obtained from unemployment insurance records from the Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD). Perhaps most strikingly, our large sample size and merged American Community Survey (ACS) data allow us to consider the earnings profiles of couples where women outearn their male partners prior to the birth of their first child. On average, women earn 57% more than men in these “female-breadwinner” families. Were couples simply seeking to maximize household income conditional on a certain amount of time investment in children, we would expect to see fatherhood penalties.* Instead, we see one of the largest motherhood penalties in female-breadwinner families. Indeed, higher-earning women experience a 60% drop from prechildbirth earnings relative to their lower-earning male partner and the highest of our various sample stratifications. The pattern we find for the United States is the polar opposite of that for Sweden. Angelov et al. (4) find no motherhood penalty among the subset of families with small or negative prechild gender gaps and conclude: “the parent who is relatively better paid concentrates on labor market work, and the other parent picks up the slack at home”.

Along other seemingly propitious dimensions, the US motherhood penalty is surprisingly robust and frequently homogenous. For earnings conditional on employment, the motherhood penalty for the college-educated is similar in percentage terms to the non-college-educated (and larger in absolute terms for college-educated mothers). Similarly, if we look at particular firms, we might expect to be motherhood-friendly—those at which more than 75% of the employees are female or even the 17% of firms where the boss is female—we find an undiminished motherhood penalty. If anything, this motherhood penalty grows faster over time at firms headed by women. On the whole, our findings are discouraging even relative to the existing work on motherhood penalties.

Previous work on the US motherhood penalty has relied exclusively on survey data. We use administrative data from the Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) database, arguably the best US dataset available for estimating motherhood penalties. The LEHD data contain the universe of job histories at the employer–employee–quarter level for every individual who appears in the Unemployment Insurance (UI) program. The LEHD reports earnings by quarter, which allows us to evaluate the labor market dynamics around childbirth. We obtained these data for 22 US States and Washington, DC. The LEHD also provides orders of magnitude more “sample” size than previously analyzed survey data of annual data on US employment and earnings, e.g., the PSID used in Kleven et al. (7) and the SIPP in Goldin and Mitchell (5).† Our employment and earnings data run for 22 y (from Q1 1990 to Q1 2012).‡ While the LEHD data have been used to examine the long-term effects of early-childhood pollution (10), earnings inequality and mobility (11), and other core topics in labor economics, we know of no published research on the motherhood penalty using these administrative data.§ This is likely because the LEHD is comparatively difficult for researchers to gain access to and must be analyzed on site at the US Census Bureau or a US Census Research Data Center.

These LEHD data allow us to consider labor market outcomes from 5 y before childbirth to 6 y after childbirth. Overall, in these data, we find that women experience a $2,000 decrease in quarterly earnings relative to men upon becoming parents or roughly half of women’s prechildbirth earnings. Both the extensive margin of labor force participation and intensive margin (earnings of the employed) contribute to the penalty, and moreover, this average motherhood penalty tends to grow over time. These penalty estimates are similar qualitatively to those in Kleven et al. (7) and, if anything, somewhat larger.

Beyond providing administrative earnings records for the universe of workers in 22 states, the LEHD data reveal more about the firm and family context for the motherhood penalty. On the firm side, the LEHD allows us to link employees to unique firm IDs. This in turn allows us to compare across and within firms¶ while evaluating the motherhood penalty. Observing the universe of UI records in participating states is what allows us to construct measures of the work environment for each employee, including the fraction of female employees at each firm and whether one’s boss (defined as the top earner) is male or female. By merging the LEHD data to the ACS,# we add additional background characteristics of the employee. Chief among them is the quarter in which the employee’s child was born. Additionally, we merge in information on educational attainment of the parent and a unique identifier for the other parent. These features of our unique dataset provide the data elements and statistical power to isolate specific situations where the motherhood penalty might be expected to fall, while controlling for unobserved sources of differences across couples. The LEHD data allow us to decompose the motherhood penalty into proximate sources, including the extent to which motherhood precludes job transitions to higher-paying firms.

Across the board, our results suggest that the motherhood penalty is robust, large, and perhaps surprisingly homogenous. Might this suggest the motherhood penalty is immutable? To shed light on immutability, Kleven et al. (3) consider whether biology drives motherhood penalties, e.g., through persistent effects of pregnancy itself or breastfeeding. They reject a biological explanation, as do Andresen and Nix (8), who compare motherhood impacts among heterosexual parents in Norway to those in same-sex female couples.

Doubts on biological explanations together with tremendous progress along other key dimensions (e.g., female educational attainment in the US) might appear encouraging for the prospects of eliminating the motherhood penalty. But the large penalties we find among subpopulations where we might expect them to be attenuated suggest the difficulty of “easy wins” by which motherhood penalty might be reduced or eliminated. Strikingly, women outearning men in the United States are insufficient to alter the within-couple dynamic so as to generate fatherhood penalties, as rudimentary economic theory would predict. Instead, and given the impotence of biological explanations, we suspect the gendered culture and social norms surrounding parenting would need to change if the large motherhood penalties are to be eliminated. While challenging, changes to established norms are not unprecedented and may hold more promise in reducing the motherhood penalty than proximate policy tools, like “family-friendly” worker policies.

Brief Literature Review

Seminal work by Waldfogel (12) identified substantial motherhood penalties in NLSY data. Below we give a very brief summary of the literature on the motherhood penalty.

The recent upsurge of researcher interest in the motherhood penalty is driven by two factors. First, the availability of administrative databases on earnings has increased, e.g., Angelov et al.’s (4) analysis of Swedish register data. Second, motherhood now accounts for the vast majority of the overall gender earnings gap, as argued by Kleven et al. (3). While this reflects in part progress on other dimensions, such as educational attainment, the delay of motherhood in more recent cohorts means that women’s careers are increasingly interrupted by childbirth (5).

In using the timing of child arrival as a natural experiment for the motherhood penalty, Kleven et al. (1) note that the “event study approach cannot capture the prechild effect; it is designed to identify only the postchild effect.” Thus, factors like reduced investment by women in their careers in anticipation of a motherhood penalty are not captured with these analyses. Still, that the motherhood penalty accounts for the vast majority of the gender earnings gap means that there is little “room” left for such factors (1). Consistent with this decomposition, Kuziemko et al. (6) argue using US survey data that women do not anticipate the motherhood penalty.

Angelov et al. (4) look at the within-couple gender pay gap in Sweden and how it expands with the birth of a first child. Fifteen years after the first child, the gender gap in income increases by 32% and the wage gap by 10%. After discussing models where bargaining power is determined by comparative advantages in the labor market (13), Angelov et al. (4) consider the subset of couples with a small or even negative prechild gender gap, finding that childbirth does not increase the gender gap for these couples.

Kleven et al. (7) analyze motherhood penalties in six countries. Their takeaway finding is that Scandinavia seems to have smaller (though still nontrivial) penalties and German-speaking countries larger motherhood penalties. With a motherhood penalty of around 40%, the United States falls somewhere in the middle internationally. While we will find a slightly higher average mother penalty than Kleven et al. (7), we consider births from 1995 to 2004, while Kleven et al. (7) use 1967 to 2006 births (and PSID data from all states).

Finally, Cortes and Pan (9) provide an overview of the recent literature on children and gender gaps in the labor market. Using the PSID, they compare mothers with higher educational attainment than fathers and find a motherhood penalty in those families. They state that in such families, mothers “likely (have) higher market wages” but do not use wages for stratification. As noted above, their baseline model predicts no motherhood penalty when the mother outearns the father. When mothers receive higher wages than their male partners, “then motherhood earnings penalties can only be rationalized if women are more productive at home, value the household good more, or households incur a utility penalty from women working in the market.”

Methodology

We consider labor market outcomes in an 11-y window from 5 y before an employee’s first childbirth to 6 y after it using the merged LEHD–ACS data. To construct a balanced employment data panel, we restrict our sample to 1995 to 2004 childbirth cohorts so that their parents’ employment history panel starts from 1990 and ends by 2010. We further exclude individuals whose 11-y employment window extends outside their states’ LEHD data periods.‖ This leads to our baseline sample of 572,000 births 1995 to 2004 linked to 811,000 parents (410,000 female and 401,000 male).** Parents’ labor market outcomes are measured during 1990 to 2010, constructed as a 45-quarter balanced earnings panel from 20 quarters (5 y) before one’s first childbirth to 24 quarters (6 y) after it. Fifty-nine percent of parents in the sample can be paired into couples, while the remaining 41% (21% as female and 20% as male) do not have another household member identified as parent in the ACS data.

We further consider a subsample of individuals who were employed before their first childbirths, which we refer to as the “prechildbirth employed sample”. Specifically, we consider individuals with positive earnings during any quarter falling 8 to 5 quarters prior to the childbirth events, i.e., approximately one year before pregnancy. This prechildbirth employed sample consists of 358,000 births during 1995 to 2004 and 514,000 parents among whom 265,000 were female and 249,000 male. This sample restriction is important for two reasons. First, it corresponds to a sample of individuals whose benefit of a continued employment relation is higher. Second, we are able to examine childbirth effects for these individuals along (gender-related) firm characteristics we construct. Within the prechildbirth employed sample, we additionally consider births in the subsample of households where we identify a male–female parent couple and we have employment information of both parents. This couple birth sample amounts to 156,000 births and 312,000 parents (half male and half female). This allows us to investigate the effect of parenthood considering within-family male–female relative earnings power.

Table 1 shows the summary statistics measured in the eighth quarter before childbirth, the quarter we take as the prechildbirth benchmark quarter. Panel 1 reports gender-specific prechildbirth average labor market outcomes in the baseline sample. Consistent with the labor market gender inequality literature, female employees on average earn $1,309 less than male employees each quarter, equivalent to 24% of male prechildbirth quarterly earnings. However, the gender gap is reversed when looking at the extensive margin alone: women are more likely to be employed than men. Hence, the overall deficit in female earnings is entirely driven by the intensive margin: female employees earn $2620, or 27%, less than male employees per quarter, conditional on being employed.

Regression Specification.

We analyze labor market trajectories of female and male employees before and after their first childbirths by estimating a semiparametric difference-in-differences event study regression. Specifically, we fit gender-specific prechildbirth and postchildbirth linear time trends to employees’ labor market outcomes and flexibly estimate the impact near childbirth using gender-specific event study dummies. The before–after differences by gender in earnings slope and intercept are taken as measures of the impacts of childbirth by gender. We focus on the difference in childbirth impacts between male and female employees and obtain this measure in various subsample groups.

We define the eighth quarter before childbirth, i.e., approximately one year before pregnancy, as the prechildbirth benchmark quarter. We denote the quarter of childbirth as Qtr = 0 and the prechildbirth benchmark quarter as Qtr = −8. To make the regression coefficients more easily interpretable, we shift our regression equation intercept to Qtr = −8 so that the gender gap in the prechildbirth benchmark quarter is captured by its regression coefficient. Our regression equation is

Yit=α0+α1Pit+β0(Qtrit+8)+β1(Qtrit+8)·Pit+α2Fit+α3Pit·Fit+β2(Qtrit+8)·Fit+β3(Qtrit+8)·Pit·Fit+∑j∈[−7,3](θ0jEitj+θ1jEitj·Fit)+ϕageF/M+ϕyearF/M+ϵit. [1]

Yit denotes the labor market outcome (quarterly earnings or a binary indicator of employment) for individual i in period t. Female employees have Fit = 1. Qtrit denotes the number of quarters relative to the quarter of childbirth and takes values between −20 and 24, where negative values indicate prechildbirth periods, and positive values indicate postchildbirth. Pit = 1 is an indicator of Qtrit > −8, which captures any change after the prechildbirth benchmark quarter.

The outcome dynamics right around childbirth are captured nonparametrically. This is composed of the 11-quarter window from 7 quarters before childbirth to 3 quarters after it via the coefficients on event quarter dummies Eitj and the interaction terms Eitj ⋅ Fit, where Eitj=1Qtrit=j, j ∈ [ − 7, 3]. Thus, the postchildbirth parametric calibration is based on Qtrit ≥ 4, starting from the fourth quarter after childbirth. We also control for gender-specific age and calendar year fixed effects ϕageF/M and ϕageF/M as we assume that male and female have different underlying lifetime career paths.§§

The male and female prechildbirth benchmark outcome levels are measured as α0 and α0 + α2, respectively, and the gender gap in the prechildbirth benchmark quarter is directly captured by the regression coefficient α2.¶¶ When presenting our results in graphs, we shift male and female labor market outcome plots vertically by the α0 and α0 + α2, respectively, making the intercepts at Qrt = −8 all normalized to zero. This allows for more direct visual comparison.

Next, we derive the changes in labor market outcome slopes and levels between men and women before and after childbirth. We take these as our summary measures of the childbirth impact. β0, β1, β2, and β3 are standard difference-in-differences coefficients of the outcome variable slope for men and women, before and after childbirth. One coefficient of interest is β3, which captures the additional slope change in earnings and employment upon childbirth experienced by female relative to that experienced by male. To quantify the impact of childbirth in terms of a level shift, we measure level differences between 8 quarters before childbirth to 4 quarters after it. The difference-in-differences level change can be calculated as (α3 + 12(β2 + β3)).##

The summary measures are reported both in absolute terms and as percentage changes of the prechildbirth benchmark levels. We focus on earnings but also consider the extensive and intensive margins separately by analyzing individual’s employment status and earnings conditional on employed.

All figures display both the fully nonparametric estimates (i.e., quarter-specific averages in circles and triangles) alongside the estimated slopes and intercepts of the semiparametric approach. The figures show that a fully nonparametric approach—with event dummies for each quarter—is summarized well by the semiparametric approach. Indeed, it is difficult sometimes to distinguish the two visually.

The event study approach recovers the causal effect of childbirth on labor market outcomes, as discussed by Kleven et al. (1). Even though fertility choices are not exogenous, the event of having a first child generates sharp changes in labor market outcomes that are arguably orthogonal to unobserved determinants of those outcomes as they should evolve smoothly over time. Moreover, we find reassuring evidence that men can be used as a control group for women, as parallel pretrends after controlling nonparametrically for lifecycle and time trends also hold in our analysis.

In addition to the upfront negative consequences that women bear at the time of childbirth, we observe that these effects persist and evolve over time in Fig. 1A, B, and C. The regression estimates of the slope difference-in-differences reported in Table 2 column (2) are statistically significant. Focusing on earnings, the gender gap widens by $50 each quarter due to childbirth. That is, the gap increases by 35% annually (Table 2, 8.77% * 4) when benchmarked to the prechildbirth gender gap baseline, i.e., the estimated α2.

Maybe more surprising, despite women having a higher employed fraction than men in the prechildbirth benchmark quarter, this advantage not only gets reversed at the time of childbirth due to the large immediate decrease, it also deteriorates over time by 12% annually (Table 2, 3.03% * 4).

We then turn to our restricted prechildbirth employed sample defined in SI Appendix and demonstrate their overall earnings trajectories by gender around childbirth in Fig. 1D. It is worth noting that in Fig. 1D, we see bell-shaped earnings profiles. The prechildbirth earnings peak is a mechanical result of our sample definition since the earnings curves are conditional on individuals being employed sometime 5 to 8 quarters before childbirth. The motherhood penalty on overall earnings shows a similar magnitude, an additional decrease of $2518 per quarter or a 40% drop. The lack of an identifiable effect on earnings slope in the prechildbirth employed sample indicates that the subsample of women with higher benefits of continuing employment resume their earnings trajectory in the postchildbirth period. However, the upfront impact of childbirth persists, and the earnings penalty is never recovered.

The Role of Individual and Firm Characteristics.

In Fig. 2, we consider potentially heterogeneous responses to childbearing within the Fig. 1D subsample: those employed prior to childbirth. In particular, we consider motherhood penalties by education (Fig. 2A), restricting comparisons to be within the same firm by controlling prechildbirth firm fixed effects (Fig. 2B), predominately female vs. predominantly male firms (Fig. 2C), and finally by gender of the firm’s boss (Fig. 2D).

In Fig. 2A, we observe sizable female earnings reductions upon childbirth for both educational groups. While male earnings are largely unaffected, women experience a drop of $1,954 relative to men in the subsample with no college education and $2,774 for those with college education. These impacts, which correspond to 43.4% and 39.7% of the prechildbirth female earnings levels, indicate that highly educated women are not protected from the negative labor market impacts of childbirth. Instead, they are more negatively affected in absolute terms. We do not observe a significant difference in slope between genders, neither visually nor statistically. However, it is interesting to note that both male and female employees with higher education levels experience a larger decrease in earnings trajectories after childbirth when compared to those with no college education.

In Fig. 2B, when we control for prechildbirth firm fixed effects, we can see again a large negative impact of approximately $2,800 in female earnings. As for the slope, we do not identify any significant difference between genders. These estimates are comparable to those in Fig. 1D, which indicates that the large motherhood penalty is not driven by across-firm variation in earnings trajectories but exists instead within firm. By controlling for firm-fixed effects, we also take into account industry fixed effects. Hence, we discount the hypothesis that the motherhood penalty can be explained by males and females sorting into different industries before childbirth: our motherhood penalties “live” within industry. And perhaps even more surprising, the motherhood penalty is not due to sorting of male and female employees to particular types of firms. In Fig. 2B, we do note that prechildbirth earnings curves are less flat and the gender difference in prechildbirth earnings slopes is more salient than in Fig. 1D. The inclusion of prechildbirth firm fixed effects makes the age and time fixed effects more collinear with our event quarter variable. Hence, the inclusion of these additional fixed effects is less successful in eliminating prebirth trends.

In Fig. 2 C and D, we consider the specific gender characteristics of the workplace. In Fig. 2C, we focus on the gender composition of firm’s employees and compare female-dominant firms versus male-dominant firms, as described in the Methodology section. In Fig. 2D, we conduct an analogous analysis but making comparisons between female-lead firms (top-earning employee being female) vs. male-lead firms (top-earning employee being male). We note that 83% of employees are in male leader firms, in line with findings of glass ceilings in female professional careers. We highlight that the differences between gender postchildbirth are significant for all subgroups, again with women receiving a large relative downgrade in earnings after childbirth.

Reported in Table 2 panel 2, estimates of level difference-in-differences indicate that female employees suffered an earnings reduction equivalent to 40% or more of their prechildbirth levels regardless of whether the firm is male or female dominant. The dollar magnitudes are −$2,813 for male-dominant firms and −$2,414 for female-dominant firms. When we look at slopes, we see a different pattern between the two subgroups. In male-dominant firms, the motherhood penalty persists and widens by $50 per quarter. However, in female-dominant firms, the coefficient estimate is positive, indicating a shrinking gender gap over time. Despite this “recovering” pattern, it would take 13 y for female employees in female-dominant firms to fully recover from their initial earnings drop. Additionally, if we ignore the prebirth trends and focus on “absolute convergence” between men and women after childbirth, the slope difference is not distinguishable from 0. Indeed, the point estimate is negative, indicating absolute divergence.

Next, we look at effect heterogeneity by gender of the top-earning employee, whom we take as the firm’s boss. Again, the male earnings curves show no clear disruption at childbirth. But the female curves for both groups show a downgrade of approximately $2,500 after giving birth to a child. For slopes, we can see a more negative inclination of female earnings curves postchildbirth than that for males regardless of the boss’s gender. Furthermore, our results indicate that women are more penalized in firms where the leader is a woman. In summary, we provide evidence that the firm’s “female-friendly” gender characteristics do not protect female employees from the motherhood penalty, nor do they create a more “family-friendly” working environment as we might expect.

We also consider heterogeneity in the motherhood penalty by Hispanic ethnicity. Interestingly, the gap is broadly similar between these two groups: −40% for non-Hispanic and −34% for Hispanic. A similar pattern exists between US-born vs. foreign-born Hispanic workers: −34% and −37%, respectively. While there is a larger absolute dollar earnings gap among US-born Hispanics, the percent gap is only slightly larger because of the higher female earning level before childbirth relative to foreign-born Hispanics.

Ginja et al. (14) find that firms react differently to expansions of maternity leave according to the difficulty of replacing new mothers that benefit from an increase leave. Unfortunately, the LEHD data do not include information on worker occupation, which in combination with a parent’s own occupation could constitute information on worker replaceability. We turn instead to firm size, as smaller firms may find it more difficult to replace workers. We find only slightly larger motherhood penalties at smaller firms (fewer than 50 workers) (We considered the federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) unpaid leave policy in the United States, which applies to employees at firms with 50 employees or more. Using data on firms size from the LEHD as a running variable, we did not find a “first stage” impact of FMLA on leave taking. A possible interpretation is that measuring firm size for the purposes of state and federal mandates has not been standardized). However, when we look across 8 categories of firm size, the relationship between firm size and the magnitude of motherhood penalties is less obvious.

Breadwinner Advantage?.

We now consider motherhood penalties by the baseline gender difference in earnings. Fig. 3 shows these graphically/nonparametrically following the same specification as in Fig. 1D, i.e., making comparisons by gender of the household “breadwinner” (the higher earning person within the family). Individuals not reporting a partner in their ACS survey or having a partner of the same gender are excluded, leaving 61% of the prechildbirth employed sample as paired heterosexual couples.

It is worth emphasizing that the prechildbirth earnings baselines by gender differ significantly in the two subgroups due to our subsample restriction, although the earnings curves have been normalized to overlay each other in Fig. 3 (We also see that men in such families increase their earnings roughly 7 or 8 quarters before childbirth. We thank an anonymous referee for highlighting this). Males on average earn $11,190 per quarter prechildbirth in male-breadwinner families, but only earn $5,898 conditional on their partners earning more. Women earn $5,671 quarterly before childbirth if the lower earner versus $9,273 if the breadwinner. Male-breadwinner couples are twice as common as female-breadwinner couples.

Women in both groups suffer clear earnings decreases after childbirth. The magnitude of $5,546 for female breadwinners is striking, more than double the drop in Fig. 1D. This motherhood penalty completely reverses the prechildbirth earnings advantage that females had in the family. In addition, the adverse effect of childbirth is intensified over time by $262 each quarter, as reflected by the slope estimate. On the other hand, women who earn less than their partner before childbirth only experience an earnings decrease of $1,941. This upfront negative impact gets remedied by $48 quarterly postchildbirth. If we ignore prebirth trends, there is no convergence in earnings in male-breadwinner families. In results not shown, the absolute gap widens if anything after birth (though not distinguishable statistically from 0, i.e., parallel postbrith trends).

In order to understand the extent to which the motherhood penalty comes from women switching to lower-paying firms offering greater flexibility or other amenities, we decompose the employment indicator into three categories: employment in the same firm, employment in a different firm at a lower wage, and employment in a different firm at a higher wage. Using this disaggregated outcome measure, Table 3 panel 1 estimates indicate that it becomes less likely that women are moving to low-paying firms following childbirth. They are also less likely to stay in the same firm. Most of the earnings effect we find comes from women simply leaving the workforce. Table 3 panel 1 indicates that the increase in quitting comes roughly equally at the expense of 1) not switching to a higher-paid firm and 2) not staying at the original firm.

Table 3 panel 2 repeats this decomposition exercise for the subsample of female-breadwinner families. In these families, women are still more likely to quit after childbirth than males. Interestingly, they are also substantially more likely to stay in the original firm. The widening of the gender earning gap appears driven in large part by breadwinning women being less likely to seek (or are less successful when pursuing) better-paying jobs in addition to dropping out of the labor force.

Summarizing Impacts: Fig. 4.

Fig. 4 summarizes our key parametric impact estimates. Our “mildest” α3 motherhood penalty estimate is for overall earnings: −$1,315 per quarter or about $5,300 per year (similar effect magnitute for foreign-born Hispanic). We can interpret α3 as the normalized motherhood penalty projected two years before childbirth. Table 2 column (5) reports the corresponding “DD-level effect”, α3 + 12(β2 + β3). This can be interpreted as the normalized motherhood penalty one year after childbirth.

Fig. 4B considers how relative slopes change after childbirth. These estimates tend to be negative, indicating that women do worse over time relative to prebirth trends. As discussed above, the two positive β3 estimates reflect parallel postbirth trajectories, not postbirth convergence (which occurs relative to differential prebirth trends).

Both panels show that female-breadwinner families show the largest motherhood penalties.

Conclusion

We leverage a previously untapped source of US administrative data to consider the motherhood penalty. We find large and surprisingly homogenous penalty estimates. The large motherhood penalty in female-breadwinner families and at firms headed by women is consistent with the argument in Kleven et al. (1) that the motherhood penalty is a cultural feature/social norm. This may circumscribe the role of traditional public policies, especially leave policy, to close the motherhood gap. (Compared to other developed countries, the United States obviously has less generous leave policies, which might raise their return at the margin.) Empirically, we find starkly different results for female-breadwinner families in the United States than in Sweden, where motherhood penalties vanish (4). This might be viewed as an extreme version of Bertrand et al. (15) finding that US women who outearn husbands spend more time on household chores. Invoking the economic theory of identity (16), Bertrand et al. (15) argue that a “threatening wife” does more housework in order to “assuage the husband’s unease with the [earnings] situation”. More generally regarding gendered cultural norms, Giuliano (17) noted that “Most of the research illustrates that differences in cultural norms regarding gender roles tend to persist and that they evolve very slowly, even after the historical conditions have changed”.

As Kleven et al. (1) note, gender differences in “preferences for child care vs market work” could also account for motherhood penalties and moreover argue against policies to eliminate the penalty. In contrast, Kuziemko et al. (6) focus on realized costs and argue that women underestimate the costs of motherhood, both in the short and long run. At the time of childbirth, mothers receive an information shock about these costs (6). Additionally, mother’s attitudes on work–family balance become more conservative with the appearance of children. Kuziemko et al. (6) attribute this updating to an unexpected, secular rise in the costs of motherhood.

To the extent that culture and norms drive the motherhood penalty—including facets that are only revealed at the time of childbirth—the motherhood penalty might appear less economic and more intractable. Still, culture, preferences, and norms can change dramatically, as been seen recently for “wedge” issues such as gay marriage in the United States. Taking an example closer to childbearing, it has been argued that son preference and sex selection reflect cultural phenomena, e.g., Das Gupta (18). Korea has achieved large reductions in its sex ratio at birth by “trigging normative changes within the society as a whole” (19). Likewise encouraging, Kleven et al. (1) show that motherhood penalties in Denmark are smaller in families where the maternal grandmother was employed, consistent with “vertical transmission” (17). Leveraging German reunification, Boelmann et al. (20) find evidence consistent with both vertical and horizontal transmission of norms from peers. Culture and preferences are malleable, and changes to them may offer the best route to reducing or eliminating motherhood penalties. Future research might explore what policies and initiatives might be most effective in shifting norms of motherhood vs. fatherhood. And while child penalties would presumably continue to exist under gender-neutral norms, these can be addressed with more traditional, family-friendly public policies, particularly in less family-accommodating countries like the United States.
